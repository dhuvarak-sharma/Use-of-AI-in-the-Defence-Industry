# Qualitative Findings

The survey included several open-ended questions. These responses were not treated as though they were ordinary multiple-choice variables.

## 1. Automated combatants

The question asked respondents to compare automated combatants with human counterparts while considering physical, psychological and emotional limitations.

Recurring ideas included:

- endurance and resistance to fatigue;
- potentially greater consistency or physical efficiency;
- reduced exposure of human personnel to physical danger;
- dependence on training data and system quality;
- vulnerability to hacking or incorrect information;
- absence of human emotional intelligence;
- difficulty handling ambiguous or unconventional situations;
- concern that technical efficiency could increase the consequences of mistakes.

There were 192 substantive responses and 9 missing responses.

## 2. Trolley-problem scenario

The question asked whether AI should be allowed/programmed to sacrifice one soldier to save several.

The responses included several recurring positions:

### Consequence-focused reasoning
Some respondents argued that saving a greater number of lives could justify the sacrifice under particular circumstances.

### Human moral authority
Many respondents argued that AI should not independently decide which person should be sacrificed because human life cannot be reduced to a numerical optimisation problem.

### Conditional human oversight
A recurring middle position was that AI could analyse the situation or recommend an action, but a human commander or supervisor should make the final decision.

### Context dependence
Some respondents rejected a simple yes/no answer and argued that factors such as alternatives, certainty, the identity or role of those involved, and the wider circumstances would matter.

## 3. Inviolable boundaries

The open-ended boundary question produced particularly strong recurrence around:

- autonomous lethal decision-making;
- taking human life without human authorization;
- unrestricted autonomy;
- loss of human control;
- privacy violations;
- access to sensitive national-security information;
- uncontrolled escalation;
- decisions involving nuclear or mass-casualty consequences;
- hacking and manipulation.

## 4. Additional perspectives

The final optional question contained many short comments as well as longer reflections. Common subjects included:

- the need for human oversight;
- cybersecurity;
- privacy;
- regulation;
- responsible AI development;
- the possibility of using AI to support rather than replace human judgment;
- concerns about misuse;
- calls for further research.

## Interpretation note

These themes are descriptive interpretations of the written responses. They should not be treated as statistically representative categories unless a future version of the study applies a formal coding protocol with independently reviewed coding decisions.

The repository deliberately does not reproduce all individual responses because the original survey collected names and the participants were reached through school and personal networks.

# Limitations

# Limitations

## Sampling limitations

The survey used voluntary convenience sampling. Respondents were reached through school students/classmates, WhatsApp and personal contacts. The resulting sample cannot be treated as a probability sample or as a statistically representative sample of the wider population.

## Demographic composition

The 17–20 age group is the largest group in the sample. Differences between age groups therefore need to be interpreted in the context of unequal group sizes.

## Self-reporting

The survey records stated opinions. It does not independently measure respondents' technical expertise, professional defence experience or actual behaviour.

## Hypothetical scenarios

Several questions concern hypothetical military and ethical scenarios. Real-world decision-making involves contextual, legal and operational information that cannot be reproduced fully in a short survey.

## Open-ended questions

Open-ended responses are rich but difficult to reduce to a single numerical measure. The repository therefore reports recurring themes rather than inventing numerical scales for questions that did not use them.

## Missing responses

The automated-combatant question contains nine missing entries in the exported data despite being marked required in the form. The optional final-comments question contains many missing entries.

## Survey wording

Some questions combine technical, ethical and legal concepts. Different respondents may therefore have interpreted the same hypothetical scenario differently.

## Generalisability

The conclusions of this project should be understood as conclusions about this survey sample and project context, not as claims about the opinions of all citizens, military personnel, policymakers or AI researchers.

# Reflection and Conclusion

The survey on AI in defence produced a picture of respondents who were aware of AI's growing significance while remaining cautious about unrestricted autonomy.

A substantial majority of the sample agreed that AI and autonomous systems are likely to play an important role in future conflicts. At the same time, cybersecurity vulnerability emerged as the most frequently selected predefined risk, and international regulation received strong support.

The open-ended questions added an important dimension to the numerical results. Respondents repeatedly discussed human judgment, moral responsibility, privacy, accountability and the danger of allowing a machine to make irreversible decisions without human oversight.

The trolley-problem question was especially revealing because it showed that people can recognise the mathematical appeal of minimising casualties while still questioning whether an algorithm should be granted the moral authority to decide which human life should be sacrificed.

The project therefore concludes that the survey sample generally viewed AI as a potential aid to defence rather than as a complete replacement for human judgment. The findings also suggest that technical capability alone is not sufficient: the use of AI in sensitive defence contexts raises questions about who remains responsible, what safeguards are required, and where human authority should remain decisive.

These conclusions apply to the respondents and context of this project. They are not intended to represent the views of an entire population.
